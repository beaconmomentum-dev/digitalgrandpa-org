# AI & Technology: A Blessing or a Crutch for the Next Generation?

At 65, I've lived through more technological revolutions than most people see in three lifetimes. I watched the first computers fill entire rooms, saw the internet transform from a military experiment to the backbone of civilization, and now I'm witnessing artificial intelligence reshape how we think, work, and relate to each other.

The young people ask me: "Bob, is AI going to replace us? Should we be afraid?"

The older folks worry: "Are we raising a generation that can't think for themselves?"

Both questions miss the point. The real question isn't whether AI is good or bad—it's whether we're teaching the next generation to use it as a tool for growth or a substitute for thinking. And from where I sit, that answer depends entirely on the wisdom we pass down.

## The Pattern of Every Technological Revolution

I've seen this dance before. When calculators arrived, teachers said kids would never learn math. When GPS became common, people worried we'd all get lost without it. When smartphones emerged, everyone predicted the death of human memory and social connection.

Were they wrong? Not entirely. Some people did become over-dependent. But others used these tools to reach heights that were previously impossible.

The pattern is always the same: new technology arrives, fear spreads, then we adapt and find ways to use it that enhance rather than replace human capability. The key is in how we approach the adaptation.

## What I've Learned About AI and Young People

Through my work with at-risk youth, I've watched teenagers use AI in ways that would astonish their critics—and concern their parents.

I've seen a 17-year-old who struggled with writing use AI to help structure his thoughts, then craft a college application essay that brought tears to my eyes. Not because AI wrote it, but because it helped him find his own voice.

I've watched a young veteran use AI to practice job interviews, working through scenarios until he gained the confidence to walk into that hiring manager's office and speak his truth.

I've seen former gang members use AI coding assistants to build apps that help other young people avoid the mistakes they made.

But I've also seen the other side. Kids who let AI do their thinking for them. Young adults who've forgotten how to struggle through problems, how to sit with uncertainty, how to develop their own judgment.

The difference isn't the technology. It's the foundation they were given before they encountered it.

## The Real Danger Isn't AI—It's Wisdom Deficit

Here's what concerns me: we're so busy arguing about AI that we're neglecting the deeper problem. Too many young people are arriving at adulthood without the basic wisdom tools they need to navigate any challenge, technological or otherwise.

They don't know how to:
- Think critically about information
- Sit with discomfort long enough to learn from it
- Distinguish between convenience and growth
- Understand that struggle builds strength
- Recognize that easy answers are usually wrong answers

These aren't technology problems. These are wisdom problems. And wisdom can't be downloaded, purchased, or automated. It has to be lived, earned, and passed down from one generation to the next.

## How to Raise AI-Native Kids Who Still Think

After eight years of working with young people navigating a digital world, here's what I've learned about raising kids who can harness AI without being enslaved by it:

### 1. Teach Them to Question Everything

Before they learn to prompt an AI, teach them to question their own assumptions. Before they ask a computer for answers, teach them to ask better questions. The quality of what comes out depends entirely on the quality of what goes in—and that includes the human doing the asking.

### 2. Make Them Struggle First

When my grandson asks me to help with his homework, I don't give him answers. I make him work through the problem until he's genuinely stuck. Only then do we explore what tools—AI or otherwise—might help him break through.

The struggle isn't the enemy of learning. It's the foundation of learning. AI should amplify the thinking they've already done, not replace the thinking they haven't started.

### 3. Show Them the Difference Between Tools and Crutches

A hammer helps you build a house, but only if you know what you're building and why. AI is the same way. It's a powerful tool in the hands of someone with purpose, direction, and judgment. In the hands of someone without those things, it's just an expensive way to avoid growth.

### 4. Teach Them to Fail Forward

The most dangerous thing about AI isn't that it might replace human intelligence—it's that it might prevent young people from developing resilience. When you can get answers instantly, you never learn to sit with not knowing. When you can generate content effortlessly, you never learn to push through creative blocks.

Failure isn't the opposite of success. It's the foundation of success. Make sure they understand that before they encounter tools that can insulate them from failure.

## The Grandfather's Test

Here's how I evaluate whether a young person is using AI wisely: I ask them to explain their thinking without using the tool.

Can they walk me through their reasoning? Do they understand the principles behind the solution? Can they spot when the AI got something wrong? Do they know what they don't know?

If yes, then AI is enhancing their capabilities. If no, then AI is replacing their development.

## The Opportunity Hidden in the Challenge

The same technology that could create a generation of dependent thinkers could also create a generation of enhanced humans. The difference is in how we guide them.

Imagine young people who use AI to:
- Explore ideas they never could have accessed before
- Learn from the wisdom of cultures and disciplines far from their experience
- Rapidly prototype solutions to problems that matter to them
- Connect with mentors and communities around the world
- Turn their struggles into strength by documenting and sharing their journey

This isn't science fiction. I've seen it happening. But it only happens when young people approach AI with wisdom, not just enthusiasm.

## What the Elders Owe the Young

We who have lived through previous technological revolutions owe the next generation more than warnings about AI. We owe them the wisdom to navigate it.

That means:
- Sharing our stories of adaptation and resilience
- Teaching them to think before they prompt
- Showing them that the best technology serves human purposes, not the other way around
- Helping them understand that wisdom comes from experience, not information
- Demonstrating that character matters more than capability

## A Blessing Disguised as a Challenge

The AI revolution isn't just a technological shift—it's a wisdom test. It's forcing us to articulate and pass down the essential human capabilities that no machine can replicate: judgment, character, resilience, empathy, and the ability to find meaning in struggle.

In that sense, AI might be the greatest gift we could give the next generation—not because of what it can do for them, but because of what it's forcing us to teach them about being human.

The young people who learn to dance with AI rather than depend on it will have capabilities their grandparents could never have imagined. But they'll build those capabilities on foundations as old as humanity itself: curiosity, perseverance, wisdom, and the willingness to grow through difficulty.

## The Choice We Make Today

Every generation faces moments when the world changes faster than wisdom can keep pace. This is one of those moments. But it's also an opportunity.

We can raise a generation that uses AI as a crutch, avoiding the struggle that builds strength. Or we can raise a generation that uses AI as a lever, amplifying the wisdom and character they've already developed.

The technology will evolve with or without us. But the wisdom to use it well—that's something only we can pass down.

The choice we make today will echo through generations. Let's choose wisely.
