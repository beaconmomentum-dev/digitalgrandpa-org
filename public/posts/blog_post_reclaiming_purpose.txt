# Reclaiming Purpose in Your 60s: Why I'm Just Getting Started

Last week, a younger colleague asked me a question that stopped me cold: "Bob, when are you going to retire and enjoy life?"

I laughed, but the question stuck with me. Enjoy life? I'm living the most purposeful years of my existence. Retire? From what—from being useful? From making a difference? From the work that gives my days meaning?

At 62, when most people start planning their exit strategy, I started building up. And I want to tell you why—because if you're in your 60s, or approaching them, this might be the most important conversation we have.

## The Reinvention Myth

Somewhere along the way, America bought into a myth about the 60s. The myth says that this decade is about preparation for decline, about stepping aside gracefully, about making room for the next generation.

I've watched this myth destroy more potential than any economic crisis.

I've seen brilliant minds convince themselves their thinking years are numbered. I've watched experienced hands fold themselves because they believed their building days were behind them. I've known wise hearts stop sharing their stories because they thought their time had passed.

Every one of them was entering their most valuable decade. Every one of them had their best work ahead of them. Instead, they accepted society's narrative that 60-something means stepping back when it should mean stepping up.

That's not aging gracefully. That's surrendering prematurely.

## Why I Started Building at 57

When I walked out of federal prison in 2017 at age 57, I had every excuse to fade quietly into the background. Fourteen years behind the wire. Professional reputation shattered. Starting over at an age when most people are planning their golden parachutes.

But here's what those fourteen years taught me: experience is the only currency that truly appreciates with time, and I was just reaching my prime earning years.

So I founded GraceHouse Transitional Ministries. I started picking people up at prison gates, taking them to Walmart to buy basic dignity, helping them rebuild their lives from nothing. I used every hard lesson I'd learned to serve others walking similar paths.

At 62, when I should have been planning my retirement, I was just hitting my stride.

## The 60s Advantage

Here's what the conventional wisdom misses entirely: your 60s aren't your declining years. They're your mastery years. And mastery is exactly what the world needs most.

You've survived economic cycles and career changes. You've navigated relationships, raised families, made mistakes, learned lessons, and developed judgment that no 30-year-old can match. You've paid dues that younger people haven't even heard of.

That's not something to set aside. That's something to deploy strategically.

The young people have energy and optimism. The middle-aged have ambition and drive. But those of us in our 60s? We have perspective. We have the long view. We know what matters and what doesn't, what lasts and what fades, what's worth the fight and what's worth letting go.

In a world drowning in information but starving for wisdom, that makes us invaluable—if we choose to be.

## What I'm Building Now: Rise & Reclaim

At 62, I'm not winding down my work. I'm scaling it up. I'm building Rise & Reclaim—a comprehensive community and support system designed to help people transform their setbacks into comebacks.

This isn't a retirement hobby or a way to stay busy. This is the culmination of everything I've learned, organized to help people long after my time runs out.

Why am I doing this now, at an age when most people are focused on their comfort and security?

Because I finally understand what I'm here for—and I have exactly the right combination of experience, freedom, and urgency to do something about it.

## The Purpose Clarity of Later Life

When you're young, you chase a hundred different dreams. When you're middle-aged, you're pulled in a dozen different directions. But when you reach your later decades—if you're paying attention—everything becomes crystal clear.

The nonsense falls away. The people-pleasing ends. The fear of failure diminishes because you've already failed spectacularly and survived. The need to impress others evaporates because you know who you are.

What's left is pure purpose.

For me, that purpose is simple: helping forgotten people rebuild with dignity. Veterans struggling with civilian life. Formerly incarcerated individuals fighting for a second chance. At-risk youth who everyone else has written off.

These aren't abstract causes to me. These are my people. And serving them isn't work—it's worship.

## The Energy of Purpose

"But Bob," people say, "don't you get tired? Don't you want to rest?"

Here's the secret they don't teach you about aging: purpose is the greatest source of energy known to humanity.

I wake up every morning knowing that what I do matters. I go to bed every night knowing that I've used my time on something bigger than myself. That's not exhausting—that's exhilarating.

The people who age quickly aren't the ones doing too much. They're the ones doing too little. They're the ones who've convinced themselves that their useful years are behind them.

Useful years? I'm having my most useful years right now.

## What Your 60s Could Look Like

If you're approaching your 60s, or if you're already there, I want you to consider a different vision of what this decade could be:

### The Mastery Years
Instead of being sidelined for younger talent, you could be the experienced guide that teams and organizations desperately need. Your judgment, refined through decades of real-world testing, could prevent costly mistakes and accelerate success.

### The Legacy Building Years
Instead of coasting on past accomplishments, you could be creating your most important work. The project that defines your impact might still be ahead of you.

### The Freedom Years
Instead of being constrained by career politics and financial pressure, you could be the most authentic version of yourself you've ever been. The 60s bring freedom to care less about fitting in and more about making a difference.

### The Mentorship Years
Instead of competing with younger people, you could be developing them. Your experience, freely shared, could save them decades of trial and error while giving you the satisfaction of multiplication.

## The Obstacles Are Real, But Not Insurmountable

I'm not naive about the challenges of building purpose in later life. Age brings limitations—physical, financial, sometimes cognitive. Health issues are real. Energy levels change. Some doors that were open in your 40s are closed in your 70s.

But other doors open. Doors that require wisdom to see and courage to walk through.

The key is to work with your current reality, not against it. Build something that fits your energy level, your resources, your constraints. But build something.

## Starting Where You Are, With What You Have

Maybe you can't start a foundation or launch a nonprofit. Maybe your building looks different than mine. That's okay. Purpose isn't about scale—it's about significance.

You could:
- Mentor one young person who reminds you of yourself at that age
- Write down the stories and lessons that only you can tell
- Volunteer with an organization that aligns with your values
- Teach a skill that you've mastered to someone who needs it
- Create something—art, music, writing—that brings beauty into the world
- Be the neighbor who checks on others, who shows up, who cares

The size of your building project doesn't matter. What matters is that you're building something that will outlast you.

## The Ripple Effect of Elder Purpose

When older adults step into their purpose instead of out of life, the ripple effects are extraordinary.

Younger people see models of aging that inspire rather than frighten them. Families benefit from elders who are engaged rather than withdrawn. Communities gain wisdom resources they desperately need. And the elders themselves discover that their best chapters might still be unwritten.

## A Personal Message to My Peers

If you're my age or older, and you've been told that your productive years are behind you, I want you to hear this: they lied to you.

Your body might not be what it was at 30, but your wisdom is far greater. Your energy might be different, but your purpose can be clearer. Your opportunities might have changed, but your capacity to contribute has deepened.

Don't let a culture obsessed with youth convince you that your time is up. Your time is now.

## The Question That Changes Everything

So let me ask you the question that changed my life: If you knew you had 10 good years left, how would you spend them?

Watching television? Playing it safe? Avoiding difficulty? Focusing only on your comfort?

Or would you build something meaningful? Would you share what you've learned? Would you help others avoid the mistakes you made and achieve the successes you've earned?

The answer to that question will determine whether your later years are your declining years or your defining years.

## Why I'm Still Building

People ask me why I'm still building at 65, why I don't just retire and take it easy.

The answer is simple: because the work isn't done. Because there are still people who need what I have to offer. Because purpose doesn't have an expiration date. Because I've learned that the meaning of life isn't found in taking—it's found in giving.

And because I refuse to believe that my best years are behind me when there's still so much good work ahead.

The question isn't when I'll stop building. The question is what I'll build next.
